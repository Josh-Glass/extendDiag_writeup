EXPERIMENT 3: SINGLE CATEGORY DIAGONAL STRUCTURE

INTRODUCTION

Experiments 1 and 2 demonstrated that coherence-based categorization emerges robustly when both categories in a domain exhibit strong correlational structure, but becomes less prevalent when only one category is structured. These findings raise important questions about the boundary conditions for coherence-based learning and the factors that promote or inhibit its adoption. Experiment 3 investigates two such factors: the strength of feature correlations within a category and the mode of learning through which category knowledge is acquired.

The first question concerns whether correlation strength causally influences the degree to which learners adopt coherence-based representations. While Experiments 1 and 2 used categories with perfect feature correlations, natural categories often exhibit weaker or probabilistic correlational structure. If coherence-based learning is fundamentally driven by the detection and extraction of feature regularities, then we should observe a graded relationship between correlation strength and the tendency to generalize based on those correlations. Stronger correlations should produce more robust coherence-based generalization, while weaker correlations should reduce reliance on this strategy.

The second question addresses how learning mode influences representational strategy. Previous research has distinguished between discriminative learning, where the goal is to distinguish between contrasting categories, and more generative or observational learning modes, where learners focus on understanding the internal structure of individual categories. Generative learning tasks, such as feature inference or observational study, may encourage learners to build richer within-category representations that capture correlational structure. In contrast, discriminative classification may focus attention on between-category boundaries rather than within-category regularities. If coherence-based representations depend on extracting within-category structure, then generative learning modes should promote their development more effectively than standard discriminative classification.

Experiment 3 tests these hypotheses using a simplified single-category paradigm. Rather than learning to distinguish between two contrasting categories, subjects learn about a single category (Alpha) that exhibits a perfect positive correlation between size and shading dimensions. We manipulate learning mode across two conditions. In the Generative Study condition, subjects learn about the Alpha category through an observational study phase where they attempt to recreate category members by adjusting size and shading values to match presented exemplars. This generative task requires attention to the specific feature values that define category membership and should encourage extraction of the underlying correlation. In the Classification condition, subjects learn through standard supervised classification, deciding whether items are Alphas or Not Alphas with corrective feedback. This discriminative task emphasizes the category boundary rather than internal structure.

Following training, both conditions complete identical test and typicality rating phases. The test phase includes the seven training items plus twelve novel transfer items arranged along two diagonal lines in feature space. Six items fall along the Alpha category's correlation line (X items), maintaining the learned size-shading relationship. Six items fall along a parallel diagonal (Y items), exhibiting the same correlation strength but displaced in feature space. Under a coherence-based account, subjects should classify X items as Alphas because they conform to the learned correlation, while rejecting Y items despite their similar correlational structure. Under an exemplar-based account, classification should depend primarily on proximity to training items, with both X and Y items treated similarly based on their distances from familiar exemplars.

The typicality rating phase provides an additional window into category representation by asking subjects to judge how well items represent the Alpha category. If subjects have formed coherence-based representations, they may show category idealization effects where extreme-valued items that strongly exemplify the learned correlation are rated as more typical than actual training items. Such idealization would indicate that subjects have abstracted beyond specific exemplars to form representations based on the underlying regularity.

By examining both generalization patterns and typicality judgments across different learning modes, Experiment 3 aims to clarify the conditions under which coherence-based representations emerge and the extent to which they can be promoted through task design. The results will inform our understanding of the flexibility and constraints of human category learning, particularly regarding the interplay between learning context and representational strategy.

Design

Experiment 3 employed a between-subjects design with two conditions that differed in learning mode. The Generative Study condition (Condition 1) used an observational learning paradigm where subjects studied Alpha category members by attempting to recreate them through feature manipulation. The Classification condition (Condition 2) used standard supervised classification where subjects learned to distinguish Alpha items from Not Alpha items with corrective feedback. Both conditions used identical stimulus materials and completed the same test and typicality phases following training.

The Alpha category consisted of seven items that exhibited a perfect positive correlation between size and shading dimensions. These items formed a diagonal line in two-dimensional feature space, with size and shading values increasing together. The training items were positioned at regular intervals along this diagonal, spanning a substantial portion of the feature space to establish a clear correlational pattern.

The test phase introduced twelve novel transfer items in addition to the seven training items. Six transfer items (X items) fell along the Alpha category's correlation line, extending the diagonal beyond the range of training exemplars. These items maintained the learned size-shading relationship and represented a direct test of whether subjects would generalize the correlation to new regions of feature space. Six additional transfer items (Y items) fell along a parallel diagonal, exhibiting the same positive correlation between size and shading but displaced from the Alpha category's line. These items tested whether subjects would generalize based on correlation strength alone or whether they had learned the specific correlation line that defined the Alpha category.

Materials

Stimuli were monochromatic squares that varied continuously along two dimensions: size and grayscale shading. Size values ranged from small to large, while shading values ranged from black (0.0) to white (1.0). These dimensions were chosen for their perceptual separability, which justifies the use of a city-block distance metric in computational modeling. An independent scaling study confirmed that the dimensions were approximately equal in perceptual salience.

The seven Alpha training items were positioned at coordinates that created a perfect diagonal correlation in feature space. Specific coordinates were selected to span a meaningful range of both dimensions while maintaining equal spacing along the diagonal. All stimuli were generated programmatically to ensure precise control over dimensional values.

Procedure

Subjects in the Generative Study condition began with instructions explaining that they would learn about a category of geometric shapes called Alphas by attempting to create them. On each trial, a target Alpha item appeared on screen along with two sliders controlling size and shading. Subjects adjusted the sliders to match the target item as closely as possible. After submitting their attempt, they received feedback showing the target item and their created item side by side, along with a numerical error score indicating the Euclidean distance between their attempt and the target. Subjects could make multiple attempts on each trial until they successfully matched the target within a small tolerance threshold. The seven Alpha items were presented in random order, and subjects completed one full cycle through all seven items during the study phase.

Subjects in the Classification condition received instructions explaining that they would learn to identify Alpha category members through classification with feedback. On each trial, a stimulus appeared on screen with two response buttons labeled "Yes, Alpha" and "No, Not Alpha." After responding, subjects received corrective feedback indicating the true category membership. Training included the seven Alpha items plus seven Not Alpha items that were distributed throughout feature space without following a systematic correlation pattern. Training continued for up to eight blocks, with each block presenting all fourteen items in random order. Subjects could advance to the test phase early if they achieved perfect accuracy (14/14 correct) on any block after completing a minimum number of blocks.

Following the training phase, both conditions completed an identical test phase. Instructions explained that subjects would classify items as Alphas or Not Alphas without feedback, and that some items would be familiar from training while others would be completely new. The test phase presented all seven training items plus the twelve transfer items (six X items and six Y items) in a single block with randomized order. Subjects made binary classification decisions using the same response buttons as in training.

After completing the test phase, subjects proceeded to a typicality rating phase. Instructions explained that they would judge how well each item represented the Alpha category, emphasizing that they should not judge familiarity or confidence but rather how typical or representative each item was of the category. The same nineteen items from the test phase were presented in random order. For each item, subjects used a seven-point rating scale to indicate typicality, with 1 representing "not at all typical" and 7 representing "extremely typical."

Upon completing all phases, subjects were thanked and debriefed about the purpose of the study.


RESULTS

Participants and Data Exclusion

A total of 76 participants were recruited for Experiment 3, with 44 assigned to the Generative Study condition and 50 to the Classification condition. To ensure that analyses focused on participants who successfully acquired category knowledge, we applied a learning criterion based on test phase performance. Specifically, we retained only participants who achieved at least 80% accuracy on the seven trained Alpha items during the test phase. This criterion excluded 12 participants from the Generative Study condition and 24 from the Classification condition, resulting in final samples of 32 participants in the Generative Study condition and 26 in the Classification condition.

Training Phase Performance

Participants in both conditions showed evidence of successful learning during the training phase. In the Generative Study condition, participants attempted to recreate target Alpha items by adjusting size and shading sliders. Across the seven training trials, mean error distance decreased from initial attempts, and participants required fewer attempts to match targets as they progressed through the study phase. Mean reaction times also decreased across trials, suggesting increasing familiarity with the category structure.

In the Classification condition, participants learned to distinguish Alpha items from Not Alpha items through supervised classification with feedback. Learning curves showed steady improvement across training blocks, with mean accuracy increasing from approximately 65% in the first block to over 90% by the final blocks. Participants who achieved perfect accuracy (14/14 correct) on any block after the minimum training period were allowed to advance early to the test phase. For participants who finished early, we imputed their performance on remaining blocks as maintaining criterion-level accuracy to avoid artificially deflating the learning curve.

Test Phase: Generalization to X and Y Items

The critical test phase examined how participants generalized their category knowledge to novel items that either maintained the learned correlation (X items) or exhibited a parallel correlation (Y items). We analyzed the proportion of "Alpha" responses for X and Y items separately for each condition.

Within-condition comparisons using paired t-tests revealed different patterns across the two learning modes. In the Generative Study condition, participants showed numerically higher endorsement of X items (M = 0.464, SD = 0.260) compared to Y items (M = 0.375, SD = 0.274), but this difference did not reach statistical significance, t(31) = 1.641, p = .111, Cohen's d = 0.340. In the Classification condition, participants also showed higher endorsement of X items (M = 0.699, SD = 0.333) compared to Y items (M = 0.609, SD = 0.377), with this difference approaching significance, t(25) = 1.860, p = .075, Cohen's d = 0.269.

Between-condition comparisons using independent samples t-tests revealed that Classification condition participants endorsed both X and Y items as Alphas significantly more than Generative Study participants. For X items, Classification participants showed higher endorsement (M = 0.699) than Generative Study participants (M = 0.464), t(56) = -3.019, p = .004, Cohen's d = -0.797. Similarly, for Y items, Classification participants showed higher endorsement (M = 0.609) than Generative Study participants (M = 0.375), t(56) = -2.733, p = .008, Cohen's d = -0.722.

Typicality Ratings

The typicality rating phase provided an additional measure of category representation by asking participants to judge how well each item represented the Alpha category on a scale from 0 (not at all typical) to 6 (extremely typical).

Within-condition comparisons revealed that both conditions showed significant differentiation between X and Y items in their typicality judgments. In the Generative Study condition, X items received significantly higher typicality ratings (M = 2.984, SD = 1.010) than Y items (M = 2.615, SD = 1.248), t(31) = 2.251, p = .032, Cohen's d = 0.366. In the Classification condition, this differentiation was even more pronounced, with X items rated as significantly more typical (M = 4.128, SD = 1.414) than Y items (M = 3.513, SD = 1.502), t(25) = 4.030, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.435.

Between-condition comparisons showed that Classification participants gave significantly higher typicality ratings than Generative Study participants for both item types. For X items, Classification participants provided higher ratings (M = 4.128) than Generative Study participants (M = 2.984), t(56) = -3.588, p < .001, Cohen's d = -0.947. For Y items, Classification participants also gave higher ratings (M = 3.513) than Generative Study participants (M = 2.615), t(56) = -2.488, p = .016, Cohen's d = -0.657.


DISCUSSION

Experiment 3 investigated how learning mode influences the development of coherence-based category representations by comparing generative study with discriminative classification training. The results reveal several important patterns that inform our understanding of when and how learners extract and utilize correlational structure in category learning.

Evidence for Coherence-Based Generalization

Both conditions showed some evidence of coherence-based generalization, as indicated by the tendency to differentiate between X items (which fall on the learned correlation line) and Y items (which fall on a parallel diagonal). This differentiation was clearest in the typicality rating data, where both conditions showed significant preferences for X over Y items. In the Classification condition, this differentiation also approached significance in the test phase generalization data. These patterns suggest that participants in both conditions extracted the correlational structure of the Alpha category to some degree and used this information to guide their judgments about novel items.

However, the overall pattern of results does not provide strong support for robust coherence-based learning in either condition. The generalization rates to X items were relatively modest in both conditions, with the Generative Study condition showing only 46% endorsement and the Classification condition showing 70% endorsement. If participants had formed strong coherence-based representations that prioritized the learned correlation, we would expect much higher endorsement rates for X items, which perfectly exemplify the size-shading relationship that defined the Alpha category during training.

Unexpected Effects of Learning Mode

Contrary to our initial hypothesis that generative study would promote stronger coherence-based representations, the Classification condition actually showed stronger evidence of both generalization and typicality differentiation. Classification participants endorsed both X and Y items as category members at significantly higher rates than Generative Study participants, and they also gave higher typicality ratings to both item types. This pattern suggests that discriminative classification training may have led to broader generalization gradients or more inclusive category boundaries compared to generative study.

One possible explanation for this unexpected pattern is that the discriminative classification task, which required distinguishing Alphas from Not Alphas, may have encouraged participants to focus on the positive diagonal correlation as the defining feature that separated the two categories. The Not Alpha items were distributed throughout feature space without systematic structure, making the diagonal correlation of the Alpha category particularly salient by contrast. This between-category comparison may have actually strengthened attention to the correlational structure more effectively than the within-category focus of the generative study task.

In contrast, the generative study task required participants to recreate specific exemplars by adjusting feature values. While this task certainly required attention to the feature values of individual category members, it may not have encouraged participants to abstract the underlying correlation as strongly. Participants may have focused more on memorizing the specific feature combinations of the seven training items rather than extracting the general rule that size and shading increase together. This exemplar-focused encoding could explain why Generative Study participants showed more conservative generalization, endorsing novel items at lower rates overall.

Alternative Interpretations: Similarity-Based Generalization

An alternative interpretation of the results is that both conditions relied primarily on similarity-based rather than coherence-based generalization. The X items, which fall on the Alpha correlation line, are necessarily closer to the training exemplars than the Y items under most distance metrics. The observed preference for X over Y items could therefore reflect exemplar-based similarity judgments rather than abstraction of the underlying correlation.

Several aspects of the data are consistent with this similarity-based interpretation. First, the generalization rates to X items were relatively modest rather than showing the strong categorical responding that would be expected if participants had formed rule-based representations of the correlation. Second, both conditions showed higher endorsement of Y items than would be expected under a strict coherence-based account, suggesting that proximity to training exemplars influenced judgments even for items that violated the learned correlation. Third, the typicality ratings showed graded patterns rather than sharp categorical distinctions, which is more consistent with similarity-based than rule-based representations.

The between-condition differences in generalization rates could also be explained within a similarity-based framework. The Classification condition's broader generalization might reflect a more liberal response criterion or a larger generalization gradient around the training exemplars, rather than stronger extraction of correlational structure. The presence of Not Alpha items during training may have shifted participants' decision boundaries or altered their similarity computations in ways that led to more inclusive categorization.

Implications for Coherence-Based Learning

These results have important implications for understanding the conditions under which coherence-based category representations emerge. First, they suggest that simply having a perfectly correlated category structure is not sufficient to guarantee coherence-based learning. Even when the training items exhibit a clear diagonal correlation and the task provides ample opportunity to observe this regularity, learners may still rely primarily on exemplar-based similarity rather than abstracting the underlying rule.

Second, the results challenge the assumption that generative or observational learning modes necessarily promote stronger within-category representations than discriminative classification. While generative tasks do require attention to specific feature values, they may not automatically lead to abstraction of higher-order regularities. The presence of a contrasting category in discriminative learning may actually facilitate extraction of correlational structure by making the defining features of each category more salient through comparison.

Third, the modest differentiation between X and Y items in both conditions suggests that coherence-based learning may be particularly difficult to establish in single-category paradigms. Previous research showing robust coherence effects has typically used two-category designs where both categories exhibit correlational structure. The presence of two structured categories may create a more favorable context for extracting correlations, either because the between-category contrast highlights the importance of correlational information or because learners are more motivated to find efficient rules when faced with more complex category structures.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, the relatively small sample sizes in each condition (n = 32 and n = 26 after exclusions) may have limited statistical power to detect smaller effects. The marginal significance of the X versus Y difference in the Classification condition's test phase data (p = .075) suggests that a larger sample might reveal more robust differentiation.

Second, the generative study task used in this experiment was relatively brief, with participants completing only one cycle through the seven training items. A more extended study phase with multiple repetitions might allow for stronger abstraction of the underlying correlation. Future research could examine whether the amount of exposure to category structure influences the development of coherence-based representations.

Third, the current design does not allow us to definitively distinguish between coherence-based and similarity-based accounts of the observed patterns. Future studies could include additional transfer items that more clearly dissociate these accounts, such as items that fall on the correlation line but are very distant from training exemplars, or items that are close to training exemplars but violate the correlation.

Finally, individual differences in learning strategies may have contributed to the variability observed in both conditions. Some participants may have spontaneously adopted rule-based strategies while others relied on exemplar memory. Future research could include measures of strategy use or cognitive style to better understand the factors that lead individual learners to adopt coherence-based versus similarity-based approaches.

Conclusion

Experiment 3 provides mixed evidence for coherence-based category learning in a single-category paradigm. While both learning conditions showed some differentiation between items that maintained versus violated the learned correlation, the overall patterns suggest that similarity-based processes played a substantial role in generalization. Contrary to expectations, discriminative classification training led to broader generalization than generative study, possibly because the presence of a contrasting category made the correlational structure more salient. These findings highlight the importance of task context and category structure in determining when learners will extract and utilize correlational information in category representations.
